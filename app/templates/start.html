What a unit asks of you

  1. Read what goes wrong. Every figure in a briefing comes from a source the unit names, fetched and checked rather than remembered. If a number has no source, it is not in here.
  2. Commit three predictions, with a confidence. Before any answer appears. Being wrong is fine and costs you nothing in progress. Being confidently wrong is what the score is for.
  3. Write the detection yourself. The unit ships a fixture with a known answer nobody has told you. You write the measurement in your own editor and post the number. The app never runs your code.
  4. Review. What you got right, where your confidence was off, and the primary source if you want to go further.

What the two scores mean

Rank counts whole parts finished. It rises when you have committed every prediction in a part and posted a passing detection for every unit in it. It does not care whether you were right.

Calibration is whether your confidence carried information. Say 90 percent and be right nine times in ten and it barely moves. Say 90 percent and be right half the time and it falls hard.

This app keeps them apart and never adds them together. A single blended number would let you buy one with the other. The gap between them is the interesting part. A high rank beside a poor calibration means you are getting through the work while believing things that are not so.

Three rules worth knowing before you start

  • Only your first answer counts. A question answered after reading the explanation is not a prediction. Answer again as often as you like in Review. It cannot move the number either way.
  • You cannot claim less confidence than a guess. Pick one of four options and you have already asserted 25 percent. The form will not take less, because understating everything is the one way to flatter a calibration score from the inside.
  • A part stays locked until you finish the one before it. Every part and unit names what it builds on, and the lock is what makes that claim true. Any locked unit still opens if you insist.

After the course

The fixtures all have known answers, which is what makes them safe to practise on and also what makes them practice. A cycle has none. You write falsifiable claims about a real system, seal them into git before you measure anything, then score yourself against what you found. The seal is a real commit rather than a flag in a database, because a prediction you could edit afterwards is not a prediction.
